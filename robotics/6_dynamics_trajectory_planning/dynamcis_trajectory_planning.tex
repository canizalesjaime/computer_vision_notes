\section{Torques and Forces on Joints}

There are two types of joints: revolute and prismatic.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Revolute joints produce torque.
    \item Prismatic joints produce force.
\end{itemize}

For static force/torque mapping, the standard Jacobian relationship is
\[
\boxed{\tau = J^T(q)\,W},
\]
where
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\tau$ is the vector of generalized joint efforts (torques for revolute joints and forces for prismatic joints),
    \item $W$ is the end-effector wrench (force and moment vector),
    \item $J(q)$ is the manipulator Jacobian.
\end{itemize}
For a planar problem involving only linear forces, $W$ may be replaced by the corresponding force vector $F$.

\section{Lagrangian Mechanics (Torques and Forces)}

The goal is to find the torques or forces acting on a joint.

The Lagrangian is
\[
\mathcal{L} = KE - PE,
\]
where
\[
KE = \frac{1}{2}mv^2
\]
and
\[
PE = mgh.
\]

For the joint variables used in the notes,
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\theta$ represents position,
    \item $\dot{\theta}$ represents velocity,
    \item revolute joints correspond to torque,
    \item prismatic joints correspond to force.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Algorithm}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Determine the robot manipulator position vector, represented by the joint coordinates.
    \item Differentiate the position vector to obtain the velocity vector.
    \item Compute kinetic and potential energy:
    \[
    KE = \frac{1}{2}mv^2,
    \qquad
    PE = mgh.
    \]
    \item Construct the Lagrangian:
    \[
    \mathcal{L}=KE-PE.
    \]
    \item Use the Euler--Lagrange equation to determine the generalized joint force:
    \[
    \boxed{
    Q_i
    =
    \frac{d}{dt}
    \left(
    \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\dot q_i}
    \right)
    -
    \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial q_i}
    }.
    \]
    Here $Q_i$ is a torque for a revolute coordinate and a force for a prismatic coordinate. If a known nonconservative external generalized force $Q_i^{\mathrm{ext}}$ is separated from the actuator effort $u_i$, then
    \[
    u_i+Q_i^{\mathrm{ext}}
    =
    \frac{d}{dt}
    \left(
    \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\dot q_i}
    \right)
    -
    \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial q_i}.
    \]
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Position Vectors}

The mass position vectors shown in the notes are
\[
P_{m_1}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
0\\
q_1
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
P_{m_2}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
q_2\\
q_1
\end{bmatrix}.
\]

\subsubsection{Velocity Vectors}

Velocity is obtained by differentiating the position vector:
\[
V_m=\frac{dP_m}{dt}.
\]

Therefore,
\[
V_{m_1}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
0\\
\dot q_1
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
V_{m_2}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\dot q_2\\
\dot q_1
\end{bmatrix}.
\]

\subsubsection{Kinetic Energy}

Starting with
\[
KE=\frac{1}{2}mv^2,
\]
and, for a planar velocity vector,
\[
KE=\frac{1}{2}m\left(v_x^2+v_y^2\right),
\]
the kinetic energies are
\[
KE_{m_1}=\frac{1}{2}m_1\dot q_1^2
\]
and
\[
KE_{m_2}
=
\frac{1}{2}m_2\left(\dot q_2^2+\dot q_1^2\right).
\]

\subsubsection{Potential Energy}

Using
\[
PE=mgh,
\]
where the height is the vertical component, the notes give
\[
PE_{m_1}=m_1gq_1
\]
and
\[
PE_{m_2}=m_2gq_1.
\]

\subsubsection{The Lagrangian}

The Lagrangian is
\[
\mathcal{L}=KE-PE.
\]

For the two masses,
\[
\mathcal{L}
=
KE_{m_1}+KE_{m_2}-PE_{m_1}-PE_{m_2}.
\]

Substituting the expressions above gives
\[
\boxed{
\mathcal{L}
=
\frac{1}{2}m_1\dot q_1^2
+
\frac{1}{2}m_2\left(\dot q_2^2+\dot q_1^2\right)
-
m_1gq_1
-
m_2gq_1
}.
\]

This can also be grouped as
\[
\mathcal{L}
=
m_1\left(\frac{1}{2}\dot q_1^2-gq_1\right)
+
m_2\left(
\frac{1}{2}\dot q_2^2
+
\frac{1}{2}\dot q_1^2
-
gq_1
\right).
\]

\subsubsection{Lagrange Equations for the Joint Forces}

For a generalized coordinate $q_i$, the Euler--Lagrange equation is
\[
Q_i
=
\frac{d}{dt}
\left(
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\dot q_i}
\right)
-
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial q_i}.
\]
Because $q_1$ and $q_2$ are prismatic coordinates, $Q_1$ and $Q_2$ have units of force.

For $q_1$,
\[
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\dot q_1}
=(m_1+m_2)\dot q_1,
\]
so
\[
\frac{d}{dt}
\left(
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\dot q_1}
\right)
=(m_1+m_2)\ddot q_1.
\]
Also,
\[
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial q_1}
=-(m_1+m_2)g.
\]
Therefore,
\[
\boxed{
Q_1=(m_1+m_2)\ddot q_1+(m_1+m_2)g
}.
\]

For $q_2$,
\[
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\dot q_2}=m_2\dot q_2,
\qquad
\frac{d}{dt}
\left(
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\dot q_2}
\right)=m_2\ddot q_2,
\]
and
\[
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial q_2}=0.
\]
Thus,
\[
\boxed{Q_2=m_2\ddot q_2}.
\]

If $F_1$ and $F_2$ denote the actuator forces and there are no other nonconservative generalized forces, then
\[
\boxed{F_1=Q_1},
\qquad
\boxed{F_2=Q_2}.
\]
Hence,
\[
\boxed{
F_1=(m_1+m_2)(\ddot q_1+g)
}
\]
and
\[
\boxed{
F_2=m_2\ddot q_2
}.
\]

The terms $F_1q_1$ and $F_2q_2$ that appeared in the handwritten derivation are not subtracted directly in the Euler--Lagrange equation when $F_1$ and $F_2$ are actuator forces. A product such as $Fq$ has units of work/energy, not force. External nonconservative forces instead enter as generalized forces on the right-hand side of the Euler--Lagrange equation.

\section{Trajectory Planning and Generation}

A trajectory is the time history of position, velocity, and acceleration for each degree of freedom (DOF).

Important considerations include
\begin{itemize}
    \item run time,
    \item path update rate (approximately $60$--$2000\,\mathrm{Hz}$),
    \item smooth motion in order to minimize wear and tear,
    \item via points, which are auxiliary/intermediate points between the initial and final positions.
\end{itemize}

Trajectory planning can be viewed as an optimization problem because there are many possible ways to move from an initial position to a final position.

\subsection{Path Generation Methods}

\subsubsection{Joint-Space Schemes}

Joint-space schemes use functions of the joint angles. Each via point, including the initial and final points, is converted to a set of joint angles using inverse kinematics.

\subsubsection{Cartesian Schemes}

Cartesian schemes directly use the position and orientation of the robot frames together with path points specified by the user to generate the trajectory.

In general, the notes state that joint-space schemes are easier to use and compute because Cartesian schemes require inverse kinematics at every point along the path during the path update, increasing the computational burden on the robot.

\subsection{Joint-Space Schemes}

The notes divide joint-space schemes into two methods:
\begin{itemize}
    \item cubic polynomials (or even higher-order polynomials),
    \item parabolic blends.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Cubic Polynomials}

The idea is to find a third-degree polynomial satisfying the trajectory constraints.

A standard point-to-point cubic trajectory uses four boundary constraints:
\[
\theta(t_0)=\theta_0,
\qquad
\dot\theta(t_0)=\dot\theta_0,
\]
\[
\theta(t_f)=\theta_f,
\qquad
\dot\theta(t_f)=\dot\theta_f.
\]
For the common case in which the joint starts and stops at rest,
\[
\dot\theta_0=\dot\theta_f=0.
\]

A cubic polynomial has four coefficients, so four independent constraints are sufficient to determine them. If via points are added, the degree of the polynomial can be increased by adding additional constraints.

The position function is
\[
\boxed{
\theta(t)=a_0+a_1t+a_2t^2+a_3t^3
}.
\]

The velocity is
\[
\boxed{
\dot\theta(t)=a_1+2a_2t+3a_3t^2
}.
\]

The acceleration is
\[
\boxed{
\ddot\theta(t)=2a_2+6a_3t
}.
\]

\subsubsection{Cubic Polynomial Worked Example}

The image shows a motion through three points
\[
A\rightarrow B\rightarrow C.
\]

For point $A$,
\[
\theta_0=2^\circ,
\qquad
\dot\theta_0=0.
\]

The via point is shown at approximately
\[
\theta_v=15^\circ.
\]

For point $C$,
\[
\theta_f=20^\circ,
\qquad
\dot\theta_f=0.
\]

The diagram shows a time interval of $2$ for each segment.

\paragraph{Segment $A\rightarrow B$.}

Using
\[
\theta(t)=a_0+a_1t+a_2t^2+a_3t^3,
\]
the initial position condition gives
\[
\theta(0)
=
a_0+a_1(0)+a_2(0)^2+a_3(0)^3
=2,
\]
so
\[
a_0=2.
\]

The initial velocity condition gives
\[
\dot\theta(0)
=
a_1+2a_2(0)+3a_3(0)^2
=0,
\]
so
\[
a_1=0.
\]

At $t=2$,
\[
\theta(2)
=
2+a_2(2)^2+a_3(2)^3
=15,
\]
which gives
\[
4a_2+8a_3=13.
\]

The velocity condition shown in the image gives
\[
\dot\theta(2)
=
2a_2(2)+3a_3(2)^2,
\]
leading to
\[
4a_2+12a_3=0.
\]

Solving the equations gives the coefficients shown in the notes:
\[
a_0=2,
\qquad
a_1=0,
\qquad
a_2=9.75,
\qquad
a_3=-3.25.
\]

Therefore,
\[
\boxed{
\theta(t)=2+9.75t^2-3.25t^3
}
\qquad (A\rightarrow B).
\]

\paragraph{Segment $B\rightarrow C$.}

The coefficients shown for the second segment are
\[
b_0=15,
\qquad
b_1=0,
\qquad
b_2=3.75,
\qquad
b_3=-1.25.
\]

Therefore,
\[
\boxed{
\theta(t)=15+3.75t^2-1.25t^3
}
\qquad (B\rightarrow C).
\]

\subsection{Parabolic Blends}

The notes state that parabolic blends are used to solve the problem of discontinuous velocities.

The blend uses constant acceleration, and the two blend times are shown as equal:
\[
t_b=t_{\text{blend 1}}=t_{\text{blend 2}}.
\]

For the first blend,
\[
\boxed{
\theta_b
=
\theta_0
+
\frac{1}{2}\ddot\theta\,t_b^2
}.
\]

The blend time is shown as
\[
\boxed{
t_b
=
\frac{t_f}{2}
-
\frac{
\sqrt{
\ddot\theta^{\,2}t_f^2
-
4\ddot\theta(\theta_f-\theta_0)
}
}{
2\ddot\theta
}
}.
\]

The acceleration constraint shown in the image is
\[
\boxed{
|\ddot\theta|
\geq
\frac{4|\theta_f-\theta_0|}{t_f^2}
}.
\]

The trajectory is divided into three regions: the first parabolic blend, a linear section, and the second parabolic blend.

\end{tikzpicture}

The first blend is
\[
\theta(t)
=
\theta_0
+
\frac{1}{2}\ddot\theta t^2,
\qquad
0<t<t_b.
\]

The velocity at the end of the first blend is
\[
\dot\theta_b=\ddot\theta\,t_b,
\]
and the position there is
\[
\theta_b=\theta_0+\frac{1}{2}\ddot\theta\,t_b^2.
\]
To maintain position continuity, the linear section is
\[
\theta(t)
=
\theta_b
+
\dot\theta_b(t-t_b),
\qquad
t_b<t<t_f-t_b.
\]

The second blend is
\[
\theta(t)
=
\theta_f
-
\frac{1}{2}\ddot\theta(t-t_f)^2,
\qquad
t_f-t_b<t<t_f.
\]

Thus, the piecewise trajectory written in the image is
\[
\boxed{
\theta(t)
=
\begin{cases}
\theta_0+\dfrac{1}{2}\ddot\theta t^2,
&0<t<t_b,\\[8pt]
\theta_b+\dot\theta_b(t-t_b),
&t_b<t<t_f-t_b,\\[8pt]
\theta_f-\dfrac{1}{2}\ddot\theta(t-t_f)^2,
&t_f-t_b<t<t_f.
\end{cases}
}
\]
